The Definition of Love in the Shakespeare Carrel

When one asks, "What is the definition of love?" in the shakespeare carrel, the answer is hardly simple. Shakespeare does not offer a single, tidy definition; rather, he circles around the concept from many angles — as smoke and fire, as a devil and a familiar, as something that refuses to alter, as something that enchants and torments all at once. The word love appears as a keyword 22 times in the carrel, and sentences containing it span comedies, tragedies, sonnets, and narrative poems. What follows is a thematic survey of the most definitional passages.

1. Love as Smoke, Fire, and Sea

Perhaps the most famous definition in the carrel comes from Romeo and Juliet, where Romeo himself struggles to articulate what love is:

"Love is a smoke raised with the fume of sighs; Being purged, a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes; Being vex'd a sea nourish'd with lovers' tears: What is it else?"
— shakespeare-romeo-48, idx 199

Here love is defined by its physical symptoms — smoke, fire, and salt water — suggesting that love is less a stable state than a volatile process, something that shifts form depending on the lover's condition. The rhetorical question at the end ("What is it else?") implies that love is precisely this chain of transformations and nothing more.

2. Love as That Which Does Not Alter

In direct contrast to Romeo's restless, shifting definition, Sonnet 116 offers love as the one fixed point in a mutable world:

"Love is not love Which alters when it alteration finds, Or bends with the remover to remove:"
— shakespeare-sonnets-59, idx 535

This passage defines love negatively — by what it is not. Love is not the thing that changes when circumstances change. It is the steadfast, unmoving star. In the same sonnet, the speaker also observes:

"In our two loves there is but one respect, Though in our lives a separable spite, Which though it alter not love's sole effect, Yet doth it steal sweet hours from love's delight."
— shakespeare-sonnets-59, idx 160

Love, then, has a "sole effect" that cannot be altered, even when life's circumstances steal its joy.

3. Love as Devil and Familiar

In Love's Labour's Lost, love is defined paradoxically as both a household spirit and a demon:

"Love is a familiar; Love is a devil: there is no evil angel but Love."
— shakespeare-loves-8, idx 349

Here love is both intimate (a familiar, a household companion) and dangerous (a devil). The paradox captures the dual nature of love in Shakespeare's world: it draws you close, yet it can destroy you. The same play also insists that love can "vary wit," suggesting love is a creative, transformative force:

"Once more I'll mark how love can vary wit."
— shakespeare-loves-8, idx 1053

4. Love as a Taking of Meaning

In A Midsummer Night's Dream, love is defined as an act of mutual interpretation:

"Love takes the meaning in love's conference."
— shakespeare-midsummer-16, idx 348

This compact definition suggests that love is not a feeling alone but a conversation — love generates meaning through the exchange between lovers. Love is communicative, relational, and dialogic.

5. Love as a Test of Purity

In King Lear, love is defined by what must not be mixed into it:

"Love's not love When it is mingled with regards that stand Aloof from the entire point."
— shakespeare-king-45, idx 151

Love, in this formulation, is undermined by ulterior motives. The moment calculation enters, love ceases to be love. Similarly, the speaker in Titus Andronicus observes:

"Love loving not itself none other can."
— shakespeare-tragedy-57, idx 1286

This enigmatic line suggests that love must be self-sustaining — if love does not love itself, it cannot love anything else.

6. Love as Peace Against All Opposition

In The Two Noble Kinsmen, love is defined as a force that overrides every kind of resistance:

"Love's arms are peace, 'gainst rule, 'gainst sense, 'gainst shame, And sweetens, in the suffering pangs it bears, The aloes of all forces, shocks, and fears."
— shakespeare-lovers-62, idx 56

Here love is a martial but pacifying power: it brings peace against rule, sense, and shame. It even sweetens its own suffering. Love is simultaneously a wound and a balm.

7. Love as a Life in Death

In Venus and Adonis, Adonis offers a strikingly negative definition:

"I know not love,' quoth he, 'nor will not know it, Unless it be a boar, and then I chase it; 'Tis much to borrow, and I will not owe it; My love to love is love but to disgrace it; For I have heard it is a life in death, That laughs and weeps, and all but with a breath."
— shakespeare-venus-60, idx 123

Love here is "a life in death" — an experience that is simultaneously alive and dead, that "laughs and weeps, and all but with a breath." The definition captures the breathlessness of love, its proximity to mortality, and its contradictory emotional states.

8. Love as Nature's Finest Expression

In Hamlet, love is described as the finest expression of nature:

"Nature is fine in love, and where 'tis fine, It sends some precious instance of itself After the thing it loves."
— shakespeare-hamlet-25, idx 1957

Love, in this philosophical formulation, is the medium through which nature demonstrates its own refinement. Nature is at its most exquisite when it loves, and it sends something precious of itself toward the beloved.

9. Love as Motion and Longing

Romeo also defines love through its kinetic quality — its push and pull:

"Love goes toward love, as schoolboys from their books, But love from love, toward school with heavy looks."
— shakespeare-romeo-48, idx 668

The paradox is that love rushes toward love eagerly (like boys escaping books) but departs from love reluctantly (like boys returning to school). Love is defined by its asymmetry of motion: fast to approach, slow to leave.

The closest semantic neighbor is hate — a finding that resonates deeply with Juliet's own observation: "My only love sprung from my only hate!" (shakespeare-romeo-48, idx 533). The cluster of true, false, truth, and wrong suggests that love in Shakespeare is bound up with questions of fidelity and authenticity. Soul and mind point to love as a spiritual and intellectual phenomenon, while swear and duty connect it to oaths and obligations.

Conclusion

So, what is the definition of love in the Shakespeare carrel? The answer is that Shakespeare refuses a single definition. Love is smoke and fire and sea. It is a devil and a familiar. It is that which does not alter. It is a conference that takes meaning. It is peace against all opposition. It is a life in death. It is nature at its finest. It is motion toward and away. It is most closely related to hate, truth, and the soul. Perhaps the most honest summary comes from the song in Twelfth Night that simply asks:

"What is love?"
— shakespeare-twelfth-20, idx 539

The question, it seems, is the definition.
