"Not from the stars do I my judgment pluck; And yet methinks I have astronomy"

Sonnet XIV — William Shakespeare

The Sonnet in Full

Not from the stars do I my judgment pluck; And yet methinks I have astronomy, But not to tell of good or evil luck, Of plagues, of dearths, or seasons' quality; Nor can I fortune to brief minutes tell, Pointing to each his thunder, rain and wind, Or say with princes if it shall go well, By oft predict that I in heaven find: But from thine eyes my knowledge I derive, And, constant stars, in them I read such art As truth and beauty shall together thrive, If from thyself to store thou wouldst convert; Or else of thee this I prognosticate: Thy end is truth's and beauty's doom and date.

From the Shakespeare Sonnets (item: shakespeare-sonnets-59)

Analysis

The line the reader asks about is the opening couplet of Sonnet 14, one of the so-called "procreation sonnets" in which the speaker urges the Fair Youth to marry and have children. In this particular sonnet, Shakespeare's speaker draws an elaborate analogy between the practice of astronomy — reading the stars to predict the future — and the act of gazing into the eyes of the beloved to "prognosticate" his fate.

The speaker begins by declaring that he does not derive his judgment "from the stars," but then immediately qualifies himself: "And yet methinks I have astronomy." He does possess a kind of astronomical skill, but it is not the conventional sort used to forecast plagues, dearths, weather, or the fortunes of princes. Instead, his astronomy is an intimate one: he reads the "constant stars" of the beloved's eyes, and from them he predicts that "truth and beauty shall together thrive" — but only if the Youth converts himself "to store," that is, reproduces. If he does not, the speaker's grim prognosis is that the Youth's end will be "truth's and beauty's doom and date."

The poem thus turns the language of Renaissance astrology — a serious science in Shakespeare's day — into a vehicle for the recurring argument of the early sonnets: that the Young Man owes the world his beauty's continuation through children. The "stars" are recoded; the cosmic becomes personal. What the speaker can not do (predict public events from celestial signs), he can do in the private sphere, reading the beloved's eyes as a kind of astronomical instrument.

Related Sentences Across the Shakespeare Corpus

The carrel reveals that Shakespeare returns to the language of stars, astronomy, and cosmic prediction across many of his works. Here are some of the most striking parallels:

  • Cymbeline (item: shakespeare-cymbeline-17, sentence id818):
    "O, learn'd indeed were that astronomer That knew the stars as I his characters; He'ld lay the future open." — A direct echo of Sonnet 14's conceit: the stars as a text to be read for knowledge of the future.
  • King Lear (item: shakespeare-king-45, sentence id289):
    "This is the excellent foppery of the world, that, when we are sick in fortune,--often the surfeit of our own behavior,--we make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon, and the stars: as if we were villains by necessity; fools by heavenly compulsion…" — Edmund's skeptical critique of astrology, the very practice Sonnet 14 disclaims before redirecting it.
  • Sonnet 116 (item: shakespeare-sonnets-59, sentence id537):
    "it is an ever-fixed mark That looks on tempests and is never shaken; It is the star to every wandering bark, Whose worth's unknown, although his height be taken." — Another sonnet that uses celestial navigation as a metaphor, here for the constancy of love.
  • Sonnet 26 (item: shakespeare-sonnets-59, sentence id119):
    "Till whatsoever star that guides my moving Points on me graciously with fair aspect And puts apparel on my tatter'd loving…" — The speaker invokes a guiding star, but again in the context of personal devotion rather than public prediction.
  • Julius Caesar (item: shakespeare-julius-26, sentence id383):
    "I cannot, by the progress of the stars, Give guess how near to day." — A more literal use of astronomy, but one that highlights its limits.
  • Macbeth (item: shakespeare-macbeth-46, sentence id215):
    "Stars, hide your fires; Let not light see my black and deep desires…" — Here the stars are invoked not to read but to conceal, the very inverse of Sonnet 14's project of reading and revealing.
  • Romeo and Juliet (item: shakespeare-romeo-48, sentence id1788):
    "then I defy you, stars!" — Romeo's famous rejection of astral determinism, again the negative counterpart to Sonnet 14's qualified astronomy.

Closing Reflection

Sonnet 14 is a poem about the redirection of knowledge. The speaker begins with a negation — he does not read the stars — but then performs a kind of bait-and-switch: he does have a form of astronomy, but it is pointed inward, at the face of the beloved, rather than outward, at the heavens. The poem thus participates in a larger Shakespearean pattern in which cosmic language is appropriated for personal and relational purposes. Whether in the guiding star of Sonnet 26, the pole-star of love in Sonnet 116, or Edmund's famous dismissal of astrological fatalism in King Lear, Shakespeare repeatedly asks his audience to consider what it means to "read" the world — whether in the sky, in another's eyes, or in one's own heart. Could it be that the most reliable "astronomy" available to us is not the study of distant stars, but the attentive reading of those closest to us?
