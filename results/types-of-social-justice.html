5. What Are Some of the Definitions of Social Justice?

Based on the sentences drawn from the four carrels, social justice may be defined in several overlapping ways:

  1. As a vision of an equitable society in which all members are physically and psychologically safe and secure, and in which resources are distributed fairly according to need rather than privilege.
  2. As a process of moving toward equality — not a static end-state but an ongoing, dynamic struggle that requires continuous action and vigilance.
  3. As the undoing of oppression — the systematic dismantling of policies, practices, and norms that exploit one group for the benefit of another.
  4. As the integration of redistribution and recognition — following Nancy Fraser's framework, social justice requires both economic restructuring (redistribution) and cultural valuation (recognition) of marginalized groups.
  5. As equity rather than mere equality — recognizing that treating everyone the same may perpetuate injustice when historical disadvantages persist; equity demands addressing need and providing restitution.
  6. As access and participation — ensuring that all people have access to and control over institutions such as voting, government, education, media, and social services.
  7. As a commitment to human dignity — protecting the inherent worth of every person and promoting equal opportunities for all.

7. Concluding Observations

The Distant Reader analysis of the social-justice, slavery, labor, and government carrels reveals that social justice is not a single, monolithic concept but rather a constellation of overlapping ideas, each addressing different dimensions of human flourishing and structural inequality. The most prominent framework in the corpus is Nancy Fraser's dual approach of redistribution and recognition, which insists that economic inequality and cultural misrecognition must be addressed simultaneously. The distinction between equality (sameness for all) and equity (addressing need and historical disadvantage) is another critical thread running through the literature. And the concept of oppression — understood as systematic, structural, and intersectional — serves as the primary target that social justice work seeks to dismantle.

What emerges from this analysis is a picture of social justice as both vision and process, both outcome and action. As one author puts it, "Social justice is a verb and not a noun." The carrels remind us that the struggle for justice is never finished, that its definitions are always evolving, and that the types of justice we seek must be as diverse as the forms of oppression we confront.
