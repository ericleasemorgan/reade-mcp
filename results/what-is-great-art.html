What Is 'Great Art'? A Cross-Carrel Analysis

November 2025

Introduction

The question "What is great art?" has occupied philosophers, critics, and artists for millennia. This analysis draws on five Distant Reader study carrels in which the word art appears as a frequent keyword: curated-aesthetics-other (art frequency: 273, the #1 keyword), dial (art frequency: 405, rank ~14), beauty (art frequency: 81, rank ~18), great-books (art frequency: 30, rank ~29), and literature-classic (art frequency: 10, reflecting Plato's engagement with art). By searching these carrels for sentences containing "art" and examining their bibliographic metadata, we can trace the evolving definition of great art from antiquity through modernity.

1. Antiquity: Art as Imitation and Craft

In the great-books and literature-classic carrels, the earliest discussions of art come from Plato and Aristotle. Plato's dialogues — particularly the Republic and the Sophist — treat art primarily as a form of skilled making or imitation (mimesis). In the Republic, Socrates asks: "Which is the art of painting designed to be — an imitation of things as they are, or as they appear — of appearance or of reality?" (great-books, item: plato-republic-1334, idx: id5041). For Plato, the "interest of any art is the perfection of it" (great-books, item: plato-republic-1334, idx: id363), and art was judged by how well it served moral and philosophical ends.

Aristotle, by contrast, saw art as arising from universal judgment: "Now art arises when from many notions gained by experience one universal judgement about a class of objects is produced" (great-books, item: aristotle-metaphysics-2113, idx: id11). Art was not merely imitation but a cognitive achievement — a way of grasping the universal through the particular.

In the literature-classic carrel, we find this Platonic tradition echoed. Plato's Ion poses the question: "Must the same art have the same subject of knowledge, and different arts other subjects of knowledge?" (literature-classic, item: plato-ion-806, idx: id99). The idea that art is a techne — a craft or skill governed by knowledge — was the dominant classical paradigm.

2. The Enlightenment: Art as Genius and the Beautiful

The eighteenth century saw a profound shift. In the great-books carrel, Immanuel Kant's Critique of Judgment reframes art entirely. Kant declares: "Fine art is the art of genius" (great-books, item: kant-critique-2412, idx: id1552) and "Fine art is an art, so far as it has at the same time the appearance of being nature" (great-books, item: kant-critique-2412, idx: id1539). Art is no longer merely skilled imitation but the product of a singular creative spirit — genius — whose work appears natural even as it transcends nature.

Kant also distinguishes art from handicraft: "Art is further distinguished from handicraft" (great-books, item: kant-critique-2412, idx: id1510), and he insists that "Fine art There is no science of the beautiful, but only a critique" (great-books, item: kant-critique-2412, idx: id1518). Great art, for Kant, is not reducible to rules; it is autonomous, free, and judged by feeling rather than concept.

Hobbes, in the same carrel, expands the scope: "Art goes yet further, imitating that Rationall and most excellent worke of Nature, Man" (great-books, item: hobbes-leviathan-1519, idx: id80). Art here is civilization itself — the art of making a commonwealth.

3. The Nineteenth Century: Art for Art's Sake

The beauty and curated-aesthetics-other carrels reveal the nineteenth-century doctrine of art for art's sake. In the curated-aesthetics-other carrel, the phrase recurs repeatedly: "Art for art's sake" (items: books--uc2_ark+=13960=t5cc11n53, idx: id106; books--27939, idx: id1211; books--uc2_ark+=13960=t44q7t53n, idx: id629). This slogan, traced to Théophile Gautier and the aesthetic movement, held that art needed no justification beyond itself — no moral, political, or utilitarian purpose.

Clive Bell's Art (1914), found in the curated-aesthetics-other carrel, pushed this further: "Art is simply, wholly and entirely a matter of form" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--uc2_ark+=13960=t2z31qh8q, idx: id3307). Bell argued that the value of art lies not in its content or emotion but in "significant form" — the arrangement of lines, colors, and shapes that provokes the aesthetic emotion.

Tolstoy's What Is Art? (1899) offered a radical counterpoint: "Art exists for humanity" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--uc2_ark+=13960=t8v982b25, idx: id1229) and "Art is not superadded to life — something different in kind" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--27183, idx: id171). For Tolstoy, great art is defined not by beauty or form but by its capacity to transmit genuine feeling and unite people in shared emotion. "Art is creation" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--27183, idx: id83), and its greatness lies in its sincerity and universality.

The beauty carrel echoes this tension. DeWitt Parker's Principles of Aesthetics states: "Art is expression, not of mere things or ideas, but of concrete experience with its values, and for its own sake" (beauty, item: classification-BH-gutenberg--6366, idx: id568) and "Art is an expression of life, not of mathematics" (beauty, item: classification-BH-gutenberg--6366, idx: id3179). Great art, in this view, is richly endowed with "variety, subtlety, and suggestiveness" and offers "a companionship of the imagination, a new friendship" (beauty, item: classification-BH-gutenberg--6366, idx: id3731).

4. Modernity: Art as Problem and Provocation

The curated-aesthetics-other carrel, which is dominated by modern aesthetics scholarship, reveals a profound destabilization of the category of art itself. One scholar asks: "What is the definition of art?" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: articles--aaron-from-2008, idx: id1), and another notes: "The word 'art' has many uses" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--coo1_ark+=13960=t46q2hz7w, idx: id695).

In the wake of Duchamp and Warhol, "the understanding of what art is and how it is recognized is more problematic today than any time in history" (beauty, item: search-beauty_articles-reader--prajnavihara-1474, idx: id220). The traditional criteria — beauty, skill, imitation, form — are no longer sufficient. As one essayist puts it: "Art is a complex subject" (curated-aesthetics-other, item: books--uc2_ark+=13960=t44q7t53n, idx: id5721).

Yet even in modernity, the question persists: "What is Art?" The What Is Art? title appears multiple times across the curated-aesthetics-other carrel (items: books--coo1_ark+=13960=t86h5489h, books--uc2_ark+=13960=t4dn4351n, books--16917), each time marking a new attempt to define art's essence in a world where its boundaries have dissolved.

5. The Dial: Art in the Literary Marketplace

The dial carrel, drawn from the pages of the literary magazine The Dial (1880s–1920s), captures art as it entered the modern marketplace of criticism and commerce. Art here is discussed alongside books, publishing, and the literary economy. The word "art" appears with remarkable frequency (405 occurrences), reflecting the magazine's engagement with aesthetics as a cultural force. The Dial's discussions of art span reviews of painting, architecture, music, and literature, documenting how the concept was debated by critics and general readers alike.

Synthesis: What Is Great Art, and How Has Its Definition Changed?

Tracing "art" across these five carrels reveals a dramatic historical arc:

  • Antiquity (Plato, Aristotle): Art as techne — skilled making, imitation of nature, governed by knowledge and serving moral or civic ends. Great art imitates the ideal.
  • Enlightenment (Kant, Hobbes): Art as the product of genius — free, autonomous, appearing natural while transcending nature. Great art is judged by aesthetic feeling, not rules.
  • Nineteenth Century (Bell, Tolstoy, Parker): Art split into two camps — art for art's sake (formalism, significant form) and art as social expression (transmitting feeling, serving humanity). Great art is either formally perfect or emotionally sincere.
  • Modernity (Duchamp onward): Art as a problem. The category itself is contested. Great art may be anything the artworld designates as such, or it may resist designation entirely. The definition of art has become an open question.

What remains constant across all periods is the insistence that art matters — that it is worth defining, debating, and defending. Whether art is imitation, genius, significant form, social communication, or institutional provocation, the question "What is great art?" continues to generate new answers. As the beauty carrel reminds us: "Art is one of the oldest of human activities" (beauty, item: classification-BH-gutenberg--6366, idx: id1527), and its definition evolves as we do.

The cross-carrel analysis also reveals that the definition of great art has expanded over time from a narrow set of classical criteria (mimesis, beauty, moral utility) to an ever-widening circle that includes formal innovation, emotional sincerity, social critique, and even the rejection of art itself as an artistic act. Each carrel captures a different facet of this evolution: the great-books carrel preserves the philosophical foundations, the beauty carrel documents the psychological and formalist turn, the curated-aesthetics-other carrel reflects the contemporary crisis of definition, the dial carrel shows art's entanglement with commerce and criticism, and the literature-classic carrel anchors the discussion in its classical origins.

Perhaps the most striking finding is this: the more we ask "What is art?", the less certain the answer becomes — and yet the question itself seems to grow more, not less, urgent with each iteration.
