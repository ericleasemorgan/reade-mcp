What Is Knowledge? A Cross-Carrel Reading

The local catalog holds fifty-eight study carrels, ranging from single classical texts to sprawling collections of contemporary research articles. Rather than sampling all fifty-eight, the keyword tabulation for each carrel was scanned for the term "knowledge," and only the carrels where it registered as a genuinely prominent keyword — not just present, but sitting near the top of the frequency list — were carried forward for closer reading. That left five: knowledge (a small carrel of classical epistemology texts, where "knowledge" is the third most frequent keyword), big-science (third most frequent), ai (twenty-fifth, but still notable given the size and technical range of the collection), librarianship (roughly twenty-eighth), and great-books (about sixty-second out of many hundreds, though the corpus is enormous, so this still represents real concentration). For each of the five, sentence-level search retrieved every line containing "knowledge" or a closely related term, and the results below are drawn almost entirely from those sentences, sourced from the reader tool and hyperlinked back to the original item.

What follows is not a definition of knowledge so much as a map of how five different communities of writers — ancient philosophers, early modern epistemologists, information scientists, AI researchers, and librarians — have used the word, often without agreeing on much beyond the fact that the question is worth asking again.

1. The knowledge carrel: knowledge as an open philosophical question

This carrel is essentially a shelf of classical epistemology — Plato, Locke, Hume, Berkeley, Russell, and a few later idealists like Rudolf Steiner. The keyword table places "knowledge" third, just behind "nature" and "mind," and the sentence search shows why: much of the corpus is Socratic dialogue circling the question without settling it.

Plato's Theaetetus returns to the question almost obsessively. Socrates keeps restarting the inquiry:

"Once more, then, Theaetetus, I repeat my old question, 'What is knowledge?'—and do not say that you cannot tell; but quit yourself like a man..." (item 1726, idx id1683 — source)
The dialogue tries out several candidate definitions — knowledge as perception, as true opinion, as true opinion "with an account" — and rejects each one in turn, which is part of why the Theaetetus is often read as ending in productive failure rather than resolution.

By the time John Locke is writing an Essay Concerning Human Understanding four centuries later, the question has narrowed to something more tractable: knowledge is redefined as

"the perception of the connexion of and agreement, or disagreement and repugnancy of any of our ideas" (item 10616, idx id1590 — source)
That is a genuinely different move from Plato's: knowledge stops being a mysterious relation between a knower and the world and becomes an operation performed entirely among ideas already in the mind.

Bertrand Russell, writing in 1912, reframes the problem again with the acquaintance/description distinction, distinguishing between things we know directly and things we know only by description — a move aimed at solving problems about how anyone can claim to know about objects, like atoms or other minds, never directly perceived (item 5827, idx id455 — source). And Rudolf Steiner's Philosophy of Spiritual Activity pushes the question in a more constructive direction still, insisting that

"knowledge is something which man has produced, something which he has originated by his activity" (item 55761, idx id3155 — source)
— knowledge not discovered ready-made but generated through the act of knowing itself.

So even within a single carrel of philosophers who are all more or less in conversation with one another, the shape of the answer moves: from a relation to be discovered (Plato), to an internal operation on ideas (Locke), to a two-tier system distinguishing acquaintance from description (Russell), to an activity that produces its own object (Steiner).

2. The great-books carrel: the same question, a much wider cast

This carrel folds together epic poetry, drama, and philosophy from Homer through the nineteenth century, so "knowledge" appearing 28 times and ranking around 62nd is still meaningful given how much of the corpus is narrative rather than argument. The philosophical strand overlaps heavily with the dedicated knowledge carrel — the same passage from the Theaetetus turns up here too (item plato-theaetetus-1564, idx id328 — source) — but the carrel also contains Aristotle's more systematic treatment.

Aristotle's Metaphysics makes a distinction that Plato's dialogues don't quite land on:

"For knowledge, like the verb 'to know', means two things, of which one is potential and one actual." (item aristotle-metaphysics-2113, idx id3058 — source)
That potential/actual split becomes a durable move in the history of epistemology — the difference between having a capacity to know something and actually exercising it in the moment.

Francis Bacon, writing in the Advancement of Learning around 1605, treats knowledge less as a metaphysical puzzle and more as a practical inheritance, describing knowledge as delivered by teachers or attained by one's own effort, and organizing "three knowledges—divine philosophy, natural philosophy, and human philosophy or humanity" (item bacon-advancement-2670, idx id668 — source). Thomas Hobbes, a generation later, splits knowledge into two kinds — "Knowledge Of Fact" and "Knowledge Of The Consequence Of One Affirmation To Another" (item hobbes-leviathan-1519, idx id692 — source) — which maps roughly onto what later philosophers would call empirical versus logical or a priori knowledge.

Is it a coincidence that Bacon, Hobbes, and Locke all show up organizing knowledge into tidy dual or triple categories, right around the birth of modern science? Probably not. The great-books carrel suggests that the seventeenth century is when the "what is knowledge" question starts getting answered with taxonomies rather than dialectic.

3. The librarianship carrel: knowledge as something organized, transmitted, and contested

Here the register shifts entirely. Librarians and information scientists are less interested in defining knowledge philosophically and much more interested in what happens to it once it exists — how it gets organized, stored, taught, and (increasingly) whose knowledge counts as knowledge in the first place.

A recurring theme is the idea that knowledge is not a fixed possession but something continually made through interaction. One article, working from conversation theory, describes how

"knowledge is dynamic and dialogically constructed in a conversant's encounters with his or her fellows: to be in conversation is to engage in knowledge creation." (item crl-16197, idx id61 — source)
That is a genuinely different picture from Locke's, where knowledge sits quietly inside an individual mind comparing ideas — here it only exists in the space between people.

A long 1980s piece on the organization of knowledge within library and information science traces attempts, going back to Descartes and Kant, to treat knowledge as a "structural unity," and even notes efforts across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "to see knowledge whole" (item crl-11889, idx id379 — source). The same article introduces "social epistemology" as a name for a new perspective on "knowledge about knowledge" (item crl-14412, idx id8 — source) — a framing that shifts the unit of analysis from the isolated knower to the social systems that produce and certify what counts as known.

A more recent and more critical strand of the carrel pushes back hard on the assumption, inherited from Descartes and Locke onward, that there is one universal knowledge everyone is converging toward. One piece argues plainly that the "Western view of the universality of knowledge" treats true knowledge as a single goal all knowers share, and that this assumption has historically suppressed traditional and Indigenous knowledge systems (item ijidi-39251, idx id138, id150 — source). That is arguably the sharpest turn in the whole reading: from "what is knowledge?" as an individual, mind-internal puzzle to "whose knowledge, and organized by whom?" as a question about power.

4. The ai carrel: knowledge as something to be represented, extracted, and managed

The AI carrel treats knowledge almost entirely as an engineering problem. The phrase "knowledge base" appears constantly — a structured store, sitting inside a piece of software, that a "knowledge engineer" builds by translating a human expert's experience into rules or a semantic framework (item brain-570, idx id50 — source). That is about as far from Plato's Socratic circling as a definition can get, though it quietly assumes an answer to the old question: knowledge here is treated as something that can be made explicit, codified, and stored.

Not everyone in the carrel is comfortable with that assumption, though. One article distinguishes tacit from explicit knowledge and notes that tacit knowledge, "due to its unstructured nature, remains difficult to externalize and communicate" (item jait-812, idx id40 — source) — which is really Michael Polanyi's old point (we know more than we can tell) showing up again inside a computer science journal. Another piece worries that the field conflates knowledge with mere information, observing that in the internet age, "knowledge is deemed as being in conjunction with modernity" and that a new, looser relationship between people and knowledge has emerged online (item brain-621, idx id104, id190 — source).

There's also a small but telling historical marker: one source notes that the term "knowledge discovery in databases" only appeared in 1989, coined to describe a specific technical process of mining meaningful patterns out of data (item brain-1128, idx id27 — source). That's a genuinely new sense of the word — knowledge as an output of a statistical pipeline — that none of the other four carrels would have recognized before the late twentieth century.

5. The big-science carrel: knowledge as an economic and institutional resource

This carrel, drawn from research on how large-scale, collaborative science actually gets organized and funded, treats knowledge as something produced, accumulated, and governed — almost the way an economist would talk about capital. One essay bluntly states that "knowledge production remains poorly understood" (item openalex--002582, idx id1 — source), treating it as an empirical object to be studied rather than a concept to be defined.

A retrospective piece on the discipline of information science lays out a three-part model — people, recorded knowledge, and tools — and describes the librarian or information scientist's job as managing the relationship between the first two using the third (item openalex--002377, idx id35, id37 — source). Another article on the "complexity revolution" in the sciences and humanities describes a historical split into three forms of knowledge and their separate institutional communities, tracing how the modern education system came to sort knowledge into distinct fields of culture, technology, and science (item openalex--002038, idx id34 — source), and it goes on to describe a newer "mode-2 knowledge" — interdisciplinary, collaborative, oriented to real-world problems — emerging alongside the older, discipline-bound "mode-1" academic knowledge (item openalex--002038, idx id56 — source above).

A study of collaboration and innovation puts the point most starkly in economic terms, describing "conditions of access to knowledge and knowledge accumulation as it leads to increased specialization," and treating the "knowledge burden hypothesis" — the idea that reaching the frontier of a field gets progressively more expensive as knowledge piles up — as an explanation for why research teams keep growing (item openalex--002812, idx id3, id10 — source). And a piece on science communication during the pandemic frames knowledge almost as infrastructure: "as pervasive as electricity or transport in our ability to transform economic inputs into outputs" (item openalex--002739, idx id21 — source).

So, what is knowledge — and how has the definition moved?

Reading across all five carrels at once, a rough historical arc appears, though it is worth treating this as a pattern suggested by five particular collections rather than a settled fact about intellectual history:

  • Classical antiquity (the knowledge and great-books carrels): knowledge is a relation between a knower and something known, and the central question is what distinguishes it from mere true opinion. The Theaetetus never really answers this, and that unresolved quality is arguably the point.
  • Early modern philosophy (both carrels again): the question narrows and gets more procedural — Bacon and Hobbes divide knowledge into kinds, and Locke relocates it entirely inside the mind, as the perceived agreement or disagreement between ideas.
  • Early twentieth-century philosophy: Russell reopens the question of how knowledge reaches beyond direct experience, via the acquaintance/description split, while idealists like Steiner insist knowledge is produced rather than found.
  • Twentieth-century library and information science (librarianship): the emphasis shifts from the individual knower to social and institutional processes — knowledge as something organized, classified, and transmitted, and, in more recent work, something whose "universality" needs to be questioned rather than assumed.
  • Late twentieth and twenty-first century technical fields (ai and big-science): knowledge becomes something closer to a resource or a data structure — represented in knowledge bases, extracted through data mining, accumulated as an economic asset, and increasingly discussed in terms of what tacit knowledge resists formalization altogether.

None of the five carrels converges on a single stable definition, and that may be the most honest finding here. What does seem to hold across all five collections is that each generation redefines knowledge partly in response to whatever technology or institution is currently responsible for storing and spreading it — oral tradition and dialectic for Plato, the printed book for Bacon and Locke, the library catalog for the librarianship carrel, and the database or knowledge graph for the ai and big-science carrels. Whether that counts as knowledge evolving, or just the word doing different jobs for different communities, is a question this reading can raise but not settle.
