What Is Knowledge, and How Has Its Definition Evolved Over Time?

A cross-carrel analysis using Distant Reader study carrels from the local library

Date: July 2025

Overview. Five study carrels from the local catalog were examined for the frequency of the keyword "knowledge." Those carrels in which "knowledge" appeared among the more frequent keywords were selected for sentence-level analysis. The carrels examined are: knowledge (keyword frequency: 13), great-books (28), science (61), cataloging (50), and librarianship (9). Sentences containing "knowledge" (and semantically similar words) were extracted and analyzed. The results reveal a striking historical trajectory: from Plato's open-ended questioning, through Aristotelian and Lockean definitions rooted in perception and the agreement of ideas, to Kantian and post-Kantian accounts involving synthesis and the structure of cognition, and finally to modern discussions of knowledge as organized, social, tacit, and even conversational.

Sentence Analysis by Carrel

1. The "knowledge" Carrel — Philosophical Foundations

This carrel contains classic philosophical works by Plato, Locke, Hume, Berkeley, Russell, Steiner, Prichard, Dietzgen, and others. The sentences extracted reveal a centuries-long dialogue about the nature of knowledge.

Plato (c. 369 BCE) — The Question That Started It All

Source: Plato, Theaetetus (item 1726)

"What then is knowledge?" (item 1726, idx id118)
"Herein lies the difficulty which I can never solve to my satisfaction--What is knowledge?" (item 1726, idx id1502)
"Once more, then, Theaetetus, I repeat my old question, 'What is knowledge?'--and do not say that you cannot tell; but quit yourself like a man, and by the help of God you will be able to tell." (item 1726, idx id1683)
"All this time we have been repeating the words 'know,' 'understand,' yet we do not know what knowledge is." (item 1726, idx id514)
"Knowledge was said by us to be true opinion; and true opinion is surely unerring, and the results which follow from it are all noble and good." (item 1726, idx id3247)
Plato's Theaetetus is perhaps the foundational text of epistemology. The dialogue circles endlessly around the question "What is knowledge?" without arriving at a definitive answer—suggesting that the difficulty of defining knowledge is itself an essential philosophical insight.

Locke (1690) — Knowledge as Perception of Ideas

Source: Locke, An Essay Concerning Humane Understanding, Vol. 2 (item 10616)

"KNOWLEDGE then seems to me to be nothing but THE PERCEPTION OF THE CONNEXION OF AND AGREEMENT, OR DISAGREEMENT AND REPUGNANCY OF ANY OF OUR IDEAS." (item 10616, idx id1590)
"Knowledge, as has been shown, consists in the perception of the agreement or disagreement of ideas." (item 10616, idx id2397)
"Knowledge is either actual or habitual." (item 10616, idx id1634)
"Knowledge is not got from Maxims." (item 10616, idx id2966)
Locke shifted the definition of knowledge from an abstract metaphysical category to a psychological one: knowledge is the mind's perception of the relations among its own ideas. This empiricist grounding would prove enormously influential.

Kant / Prichard — Knowledge as Synthesis and Discovery

Source: Prichard, Kant's Theory of Knowledge (item 32701)

"Knowledge, as being knowledge of an object, consists in a process by which the manifold of perception acquires relation to an object." (item 32701, idx id2236)
"In other words, knowledge is essentially discovery, or the finding of what already is." (item 32701, idx id1473)
"Knowledge is simply knowledge, and any attempt to state it in terms of something else must end in describing something which is not knowledge." (item 32701, idx id2919)
Kant's revolution, as expounded by Prichard, recast knowledge as a synthetic activity—the mind actively organizes the manifold of perception into coherent objects. Knowledge is not passive reception but active construction.

Russell (1912) — Knowledge by Acquaintance and Description

Source: Russell, The Problems of Philosophy (item 5827)

"We can, then, distinguish two kinds of knowledge which we may call immediate and mediate, or, better still, acquaintance and description." (item 5827, idx id914, cited from item 47658)
"The chief importance of knowledge by description is that it enables us to pass beyond the limits of our private experience." (item 5827, idx id526)
"KNOWLEDGE IS POSSIBLE." (item 5827, idx id745)
Russell introduced a crucial distinction: we know some things directly (acquaintance) and other things indirectly (description). Knowledge is not monolithic but comes in degrees and modes.

Steiner — Knowledge as Human Activity

Source: Steiner, The Philosophy of Spiritual Activity (item 55761)

"For, knowledge is something which man has produced, something which he has originated by his activity." (item 55761, idx id3155)
"Knowledge consists in the combination by thought of a concept and a percept." (item 55761, idx id2754)
"Knowledge has value only in so far as it contributes to the all-round unfolding of the whole nature of man." (item 55761, idx id3837)
Steiner emphasizes knowledge as an active human production—knowledge is made, not merely found. It is the combination of concept and percept, and its value lies in human development.

2. The "great-books" Carrel — Classical and Early Modern Voices

This carrel spans from ancient Greece through the 19th century, containing works by Plato, Aristotle, Hobbes, Locke, Bacon, Spinoza, Kant, Hegel, and many others.

Aristotle — Knowledge and the Knowable

Source: Aristotle, Metaphysics (item aristotle-metaphysics-2113)

"For what intermediate could there be between knowledge and knowable?" (item aristotle-metaphysics-2113, idx id2057)
"For knowledge, like the verb 'to know', means two things, of which one is potential and one actual." (item aristotle-metaphysics-2113, idx id3058)
"We answer that in some cases the knowledge is the object." (item aristotle-metaphysics-2113, idx id2669)

Source: Aristotle, Categories (item aristotle-categories-1981)

"Thus, by knowledge we mean knowledge the knowable; by the knowable, that which is to be apprehended by knowledge." (item aristotle-categories-1981, idx id235)
Aristotle introduced the relational character of knowledge: knowledge is always "of" something. He also distinguished potential and actual knowledge—a distinction that anticipates later debates about competence and performance.

Hobbes (1651) — Two Kinds of Knowledge

Source: Hobbes, Leviathan (item hobbes-leviathan-1519)

"There are of KNOWLEDGE two kinds; whereof one is Knowledge Of Fact: the other Knowledge Of The Consequence Of One Affirmation To Another." (item hobbes-leviathan-1519, idx id692)
"The Register of Knowledge Of Fact is called History." (item hobbes-leviathan-1519, idx id696)
Hobbes drew a sharp line between factual knowledge (empirical) and rational knowledge (logical). This distinction between "knowledge that" and "knowledge why" remains central to epistemology.

Bacon (1605) — Knowledge as Pyramids

Source: Bacon, The Advancement of Learning (item bacon-advancement-2670)

"For knowledges are as pyramids, whereof history is the basis." (item bacon-advancement-2670, idx id771)
"Out of which several inquiries there do arise three knowledges--divine philosophy, natural philosophy, and human philosophy or humanity." (item bacon-advancement-2670, idx id668)
Bacon's metaphor of knowledge as pyramids—with history as the base—captures the early modern ambition to systematize all human learning into a rational, hierarchical structure.

Synthesis: What Is Knowledge, and How Has Its Definition Evolved?

The five carrels examined here span over two millennia of thought about knowledge. Reading across them in chronological order, one can discern a clear evolutionary trajectory:

1. The Classical Question (Plato, Aristotle)

For Plato, the question "What is knowledge?" is itself the philosophical act. The Theaetetus offers and refutes multiple definitions—knowledge as perception, as true opinion, as true opinion with an account—without settling on one. Aristotle, by contrast, systematizes: knowledge is always relational ("knowledge of the knowable"), comes in potential and actual forms, and is distinguished from opinion by necessity. Knowledge, for the ancients, is a faculty—the mightiest of faculties (Plato's Republic)—and is tied to virtue and the good.

2. The Early Modern Turn (Bacon, Hobbes, Locke, Spinoza)

Bacon recast knowledge as a practical instrument: "knowledge is power." Hobbes distinguished factual from logical knowledge. Locke defined knowledge with characteristic empiricist precision: it is "the perception of the agreement or disagreement of our ideas." Spinoza identified three kinds of knowledge, the highest being intuitive. The early moderns began to operationalize knowledge, making it something the mind does rather than something that simply is.

3. The Critical Revolution (Kant and After)

Kant transformed the debate by arguing that knowledge is not passive reception but active synthesis. The mind imposes structure on experience; knowledge is the process by which the manifold of perception acquires relation to an object. Prichard's reading of Kant emphasizes that "knowledge is essentially discovery, or the finding of what already is." Knowledge, for Kant, is both subjectively and objectively sufficient—a product of the mind's own legislative activity.

4. The Analytic and Scientific Reframing (Russell, 20th-century science)

Russell split knowledge into acquaintance and description, recognizing that we know some things directly and others only through intermediaries. In the science carrel, knowledge becomes "a weapon in the struggle for life," an "organon" for survival and progress. The Baconian "knowledge is power" is repeated across multiple scientific texts. Knowledge is no longer purely contemplative but instrumental.

5. The Social, Professional, and Postcolonial Turns (Librarianship, Cataloging)

In the cataloging carrel, Johnson's dictum—"Knowledge is of two kinds; we know a subject ourselves, or we know where we can find information upon it"—captures the shift from knowledge-as-possession to knowledge-as-navigation. In the librarianship carrel, knowledge becomes explicitly social: it is created through conversation (Lankes), exists in tacit forms that resist articulation (Polanyi), and is embedded in cultural contexts that challenge the claim to universality. The Western knowledge system is itself "contextual knowledge situated in Western experiences." Knowledge organization becomes a discipline in its own right.

In Summary

Knowledge has evolved from a question (Plato) to a perception (Locke), from a synthesis (Kant) to an instrument (Bacon, science), from a possession (classical epistemology) to a practice (librarianship, cataloging), and from an individual mental state to a social and cultural construction. The definition of knowledge has not so much been resolved as continuously expanded, each era adding a layer of meaning without fully superseding the last. Perhaps, as Plato suggested, the question "What is knowledge?" is itself the engine of knowledge's evolution.
